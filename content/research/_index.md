I am interested in understanding how evolutionary, demographic and ecological processes interact to shape genetic diversity, local adaptation, and the evolutionary trajectories of natural plant populations. My research combines population genomics, quantitative genetics, field experiments, and historical herbarium collections to investigate local adaptation, demographic history, and biological introductions.

## Temporal Genomics and Demographic History of Capeweed

*Arctotheca calendula* | Capeweed

<div class="mjg-research-grid">
<div class="mjg-research-photo">
<img src="/uploads/capeweed.png" alt="Arctotheca calendula (Capeweed)">
</div>
<div class="mjg-research-text">
<p>Understanding how demographic history and selection interact to shape genomic variation through time and across geographic ranges is a central challenge in evolutionary biology. However, reconstructing evolutionary trajectories remains challenging in complex non-model systems because evolutionary change often unfolds over timescales exceeding direct observation. Herbarium records are an exceptional resource for recovering this missing spatiotemporal dimension.</p>
<p>Here, I incorporate population and temporal genomics to reconstruct the introduction history and understand rapid adaptation of <em>Arctotheca calendula</em> in Australia. Despite its broad global distribution and evidence of local adaptation, how capeweed became established globally and what genomic changes have occurred since introduction remain unknown. Using historical herbarium genomes, I investigate spatiotemporal changes in genetic diversity and how these trajectories have been shaped by genetic drift, gene flow and selection.</p>
<p>Beyond understanding the demographic history of capeweed, I am also building phased chromosome-level genome assemblies to construct a comprehensive capeweed pangenome, providing a genomic framework for investigating structural variation across populations.</p>
</div>

## Phenological Assortative Mating Facilitates Rapid Adaptation

*Lythrum salicaria* | Purple Loosestrife

<div class="mjg-research-grid">
<div class="mjg-research-photo">
<img src="/uploads/AM.png" alt="Assortative Mating scenarios">
</div>
<div class="mjg-research-text">
<p> Phenological assortative mating is considered ubiquitous in plants, but little is known about how it can influence patterns of genetic differentiation in natural populations. Assortative mating is a fascinating process that can influence allele exchange patterns within and among populations and whose genetic effects resemble those of inbreeding but apply only to genetic loci that are linked to the assorting trait. These locus-specific genetic effects reduce heterozygosity, inflates genetic variance and speeds up the response to selection which in turn can increase the rate of adaptation. Using a common-garden experiment of *Lythrum salicaria* (purple loosestrife) populations collected across a latitudinal gradient in eastern North America, we investigated how variation in flowering phenology and plant growth influences flowering synchrony and opportunities for assortative mating. We quantified flower schedule synchrony and assortative mating potential and evaluated how different flower-schedule scenarios (i.e. equal fitness and flowering time) influence the strength of assortative mating potential. This allowed us to investigate whether assortative mating could have facilitated the rapid evolution of flowering time clines and size differentiation observed in natural populations of *L. salicaria*. 
</p>

## Introduction History of Purple Loosestrife in Eastern North America

*Lythrum salicaria* | Purple Loosestrife

<div class="mjg-research-grid">
<div class="mjg-research-photo">
<img src="/uploads/background.png" alt="purple loosestrife">
</div>
<div class="mjg-research-text">
<p> *Lythrum salicaria* is an insect pollinated, outcrossing and tristylous wetland perennial herb that is native to Eurasia and it is now commonly found in most of the continental United States and Canada. Across it’s introduced range, *L. salicaria* exhibit latitudinal clines in phenology shaped by strong trade-offs between flowering time and size, which are constrained by season length differences across latitudes. Previous work by my PhD supervisor showed that in northern latitudes shorter growing seasons favours individuals with smaller vegetative sizes and earlier flowering, while in southern latitudes, longer growing seasons and relaxed selection favours taller individuals with later flowering (Colautti & Barrett, 2013). 
</p>
  
<p>Although it has been hypothesized that multiple introductions into North America increased standing genetic variation and accelerated adaptation, the invasion history of *L. salicaria* and the contemporary levels of genetic diversity and differentiation among populations remained unknown. Using genome-wide molecular markers I investigate patterns of neutral genetic differentiation and genomic signatures of selection across eastern North American populations of *L. salicaria*. The goal of this project is to investigate whether observed trait differentiation primarily reflects neutral demographic processes or strong environmental selection. 
</p>
